# Partition, War, and the Nakba, 1947–1949

**Status:** Introductory synthesis; incident-level detail requires dedicated sourcing.  
**Scope:** UN partition proposal, end of the Mandate, 1948 war, displacement, and armistice agreements.

## The partition proposal

On 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 (II), recommending partition of Mandatory Palestine into an Arab state and a Jewish state, with an internationally administered regime for Jerusalem and economic union provisions. The resolution was a recommendation by the General Assembly. It did not itself create two functioning states, and the proposed Arab state did not come into being at that time.

Israel declared independence on 14 May 1948, as the British Mandate ended. War had already begun between Jewish and Arab forces in the territory after the partition vote; neighboring Arab states entered the war after the declaration. The war and its consequences transformed territorial control and the lives of the population. The 1949 armistice agreements established armistice lines, not final mutually agreed international borders.

## The Nakba and displacement

The term *Nakba* (“catastrophe”) refers to the mass displacement and dispossession of Palestinians during the 1947–1949 conflict and its aftermath. More than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs became refugees or were displaced. The causes and circumstances differed by place and time and included expulsions, fear and flight amid fighting, collapse of local society, and restrictions or prevention of return. A rigorous account needs locality-specific evidence rather than a single explanation applied everywhere.

Palestinian refugeehood continued across generations, with legal, political, social, and humanitarian consequences. The right of return, restitution, compensation, citizenship, and durable solutions have been debated in international forums and negotiations. Each should be treated as a distinct question with its own sources.

Jewish communities also experienced displacement from parts of the Middle East and North Africa during the mid-twentieth century, under varied circumstances. This history is relevant to a full regional history, but it does not negate Palestinian displacement or resolve its legal consequences.

## Core distinctions

- Resolution 181's proposed boundaries are not the same as the 1949 armistice lines or today's lines of control.
- A declaration of statehood, international recognition, territorial control, and a final border settlement are distinct matters.
- General historical summaries should be supplemented by village histories, refugee testimony, military records, contemporary reporting, and demographic scholarship.
- Sources differ in framing and in access to records; cite the specific evidence behind each incident-level claim.
