# From Canaan to Palestine: Names, Peoples, and Political Authority

**Status:** Introductory research synthesis; expand with direct citations and page-level references.  
**Scope:** Prehistory through the Roman period.

## Questions

- What do the names *Canaan*, *Peleset/Philistia*, and *Palaistinē/Palestine* mean in their respective sources?
- What can archaeology and inscriptions establish about the populations of the southern Levant?
- How did political authority and territorial names change across the Bronze Age, Iron Age, Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman periods?

## Current synthesis

Human settlement in the southern Levant long predates written history. During the Bronze Age, Egyptian and other Near Eastern records refer to Canaan or related terms in contexts involving cities, rulers, trade, diplomacy, and conflict. Canaan should not automatically be treated as one continuously unified sovereign state: evidence points to a region containing multiple communities and political centers whose relationships changed over time.

Egyptian inscriptions associated with the reign of Ramesses III refer to the Peleset, commonly connected by scholars with the Philistines. Archaeology identifies a cluster of Philistine-associated settlements in the southern coastal plain, including Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, and Gath, with Gaza also central to the historical geography. Philistia was a region within the broader southern Levant; it was not always coextensive with the territory later described as Palestine.

Greek writers, including Herodotus in the fifth century BCE, used forms of *Palaistinē* for parts of the eastern Mediterranean. The name's precise derivation and changing geographic scope require attention to each text and translation. Later, Roman administrative usage included *Syria Palaestina*. These attestations establish a long history of the name, but they do not by themselves demonstrate uninterrupted sovereignty by a single political entity.

The ancient southern Levant included varied communities and languages. Biblical literature is an important historical source for some questions, but its claims must be evaluated alongside inscriptions, archaeology, comparative texts, and the circumstances and dates of composition. A responsible account neither assumes every narrative is literal contemporary reporting nor dismisses the whole corpus without analysis.

## What this chapter does not claim

- It does not equate ancient Canaanites, Philistines, Israelites, Judeans, Samaritans, or modern Palestinians as identical populations.
- It does not claim that ancient geographical names alone establish the borders or legal status of a modern state.
- It does not infer that later conquest erased local populations or their cultural histories.
- It does not project present-day borders backward into antiquity.
