01 / LAND & CONTINUITY

A landscape shaped by water, stone, cultivation and movement

Coastal plains, central highlands, the Jordan Valley and desert margins created different local economies and ways of life. Terraces, springs, orchards and trade routes connected villages and towns to a wider eastern Mediterranean world.

Ancient Jericho/Tell es-Sultan preserves archaeological layers reaching back to about 10,500 BCE, including evidence associated with early settled life and urban development in the southern Levant.

At Battir, dry-stone terraces and a spring-fed irrigation network show how generations adapted steep hillsides for olives, vines and vegetables. UNESCO describes the landscape as a continuing system of agriculture, water sharing and community knowledge.

Historical scope The name, borders and political administration of Palestine changed across periods. This project does not treat a geographical name as proof of uninterrupted modern state sovereignty, and it does not treat foreign imperial rule as evidence that local populations lacked history, society or attachment to place.

02 / CITIES, VILLAGES & NETWORKS

Urban life and rural life were deeply connected

Markets depended on farming villages; ports connected orchards and workshops to regional trade; pilgrimage brought visitors to sacred centers; and roads moved people, goods, ideas and newspapers between towns.

A Mediterranean port and major center of citrus cultivation, commerce and cultural life. During the nineteenth century Jaffa expanded rapidly and became a gateway linking Palestine to regional and international markets.

03 / LIVING CULTURE

Heritage carried in thread, rhythm, story, stone and olive oil

Palestinian heritage is not a frozen museum category. Many practices remain living, adaptive and intergenerational, continuing in towns, villages and diaspora communities.

✣

Tatreez · التطريز

Traditional embroidery uses regional motifs, colors and garment forms that can communicate place, family history and social meaning. UNESCO recognizes Palestinian embroidery as intangible cultural heritage.

Made from olive oil, water and lye, Nabulsi soap ties household knowledge, olive harvests, workshops and urban commerce together. UNESCO inscribed the tradition in 2024.

04 / PALESTINE BEFORE 1948

A society of cities, villages, farms, schools, workshops, associations and newspapers

The decades before 1948 were not a blank space between ancient history and the creation of Israel. Under late Ottoman and then British Mandate rule, Palestinian Arab society developed alongside Jewish and other communities through agriculture, trade, municipal life, education, publishing, medicine, crafts and civic organizations.

1516–17

Ottoman incorporation

Much of the region came under Ottoman rule. Administration changed over the centuries, while local towns, villages, religious institutions, agriculture and trade continued within wider Syrian and Mediterranean networks.

Research gateway · UNISPAL ↗
1800s

Growth, trade and reform

Late Ottoman reforms, expanding Mediterranean commerce and new transport links reshaped landholding, towns and markets. Jaffa grew as a port and citrus center; Nablus remained a major highland market and manufacturing city.

Jaffa research · Cambridge Core ↗ Nablus craft record · UNESCO ↗
1900s

Public life expands

Schools, hospitals, newspapers, municipal institutions, professional networks and civic associations expanded in the late Ottoman and Mandate periods. Archival collections preserve photographs and documents of everyday work, family life, broadcasting, farming and urban institutions.

Historical photographs · Library of Congress ↗ Community records · Palestinian Museum Digital Archive ↗
1917–20

From Ottoman to British rule

British forces captured Jerusalem in 1917. Military administration was followed by the League of Nations Mandate for Palestine, beginning a new political framework while the population continued to build institutions, businesses and community life.

1917–26 photo album · Library of Congress ↗
1920–48

Mandate-era society

The Mandate period saw rapid demographic, economic and political change, including growing Palestinian Arab political organization and Zionist Jewish immigration and institution-building. The era also contained ordinary life: schools, commerce, agriculture, sports, music, weddings, religious observance, newspapers and local government.

Mandate-era visual record · Library of Congress ↗ Palestinian social history archive ↗
1948

A major rupture

The end of the British Mandate, the war surrounding the establishment of Israel, and the mass displacement of Palestinians transformed the region and Palestinian society. Later sections of this research repository can document those events separately and with their own source record.

Modern political history · UNISPAL ↗
The archive restores ordinary life to the historical record.

Historical photographs and documents show bazaars, orchards, hospitals, municipal offices, schools, weddings, religious processions, farmers, craftspeople, broadcasters and families. That record matters because political histories can otherwise make daily life disappear.

05 / GAZA · MEMORY, REFUGE, SURVIVAL, CHILDHOOD

Gaza has a history deeper than the current war

Gaza is an ancient Mediterranean place, a city and coastal district shaped by trade, agriculture, scholarship, family life and repeated political upheaval. This section follows one modern thread: displacement in 1948, refugee life, occupation, blockade, recurrent war and the lives of children growing up through it.

A modern history of displacement and endurance

The purpose of this timeline is not to reduce Gaza to suffering. It records the political conditions that shaped generations of family life while leaving room for education, work, culture, play, faith, humor and hopes for the future.

1948–50
Mass displacement and refugee camps

United Nations sources estimate that the hostilities surrounding the establishment of Israel in 1948 resulted in almost 750,000 Palestinian refugees. Refugees from towns and villages in central and southern Palestine entered the Gaza Strip, where camps including Jabalia, Rafah, Deir al-Balah and Maghazi were established. UNRWA began operations on 1 May 1950.

UNRWA refugee history ↗
1967
Occupation after the June war

In the 1967 war, Israel occupied the Gaza Strip and the West Bank, including East Jerusalem. United Nations sources estimate that more than 500,000 Palestinians were displaced, including people who had already become refugees in 1948.

UN history timeline ↗
2005
Withdrawal of permanent ground presence, continued legal obligations

Israel removed its permanent military presence and settlements from inside Gaza in 2005. In its 2024 advisory opinion, the International Court of Justice said Israel continued to exercise key elements of authority over Gaza and had not been entirely released from obligations under the law of occupation.

ICJ advisory-opinion summary ↗
2007–
Blockade and restricted movement

OCHA records that movement restrictions began earlier and intensified in June 2007 after Hamas took control of Gaza, when Israel imposed a land, sea and air blockade citing security concerns. Restrictions at Egypt's Rafah crossing also contributed to Gaza's isolation.

OCHA blockade background ↗
2023–26
War, destruction and repeated displacement

The war beginning in October 2023 produced widespread destruction and repeated displacement. A UNOSAT assessment cited by OCHA found that about 82% of structures in Gaza had sustained damage by 16 June 2026. OCHA's October 2026 reporting continued to describe critical conditions, unsafe buildings, shortages and large-scale displacement.

OCHA structural-damage assessment ↗
CHILDHOOD IS MORE THAN SURVIVAL

Children in Gaza are also students, artists, siblings, friends and dreamers

UNICEF's 2026 “The Gaza We Want” initiative gathered visions from more than 11,000 children about recovery, dignity and hope. Their recurring priorities were ordinary ones: safe homes, schools, parks, health care, clean streets, trees, play and a future without fear.

Archive to 2026: places remembered, places enduring

Drag each image to move between an archival photograph and a later view. The photographs are dated exactly; the present-day legal and humanitarian notes are updated separately so an older reusable photograph is never misrepresented as a 2026 image.

A necessary legal distinction The International Court of Justice treats the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip as the Occupied Palestinian Territory. Battir and Ramallah are therefore presented with occupation-context sources. Jaffa, Akka/Acre, Haifa and Lydd/Lod are within present-day Israel, while Lifta is presented as a depopulated village-memory case on Jerusalem's western side. Those cards document Palestinian memory, displacement and transformation without labeling those present-day sites as occupied territory.
