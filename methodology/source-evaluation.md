# Source Evaluation and Historical Method

## Core questions

For every claim, ask:
- Who created the source, when, for whom, and for what purpose?
- Is it contemporary with the event, or a later account?
- Is the text an original, a copy, a translation, or an editorial reconstruction?
- What geographic area and population does it actually describe?
- Does the source directly state the claim, or is the claim an inference?
- Can independent evidence corroborate it?
- What evidence would challenge the interpretation?
- What is unknown or missing?

## Historical terminology

Record terms in the language and context of the source. Explain transliteration and translation choices. “Canaan,” “Philistia,” “Palestine,” “Judea,” “Syria Palaestina,” Ottoman administrative units, “Mandatory Palestine,” and the modern State of Palestine refer to different kinds of geographic, cultural, administrative, or political entities across different periods. Their scope is not constant.

## Sovereignty and political status

Do not infer uninterrupted state sovereignty solely from a place-name. Equally, do not infer that a population has no historical connection or rights because it lived under successive empires or did not maintain a modern state continuously. For each period, specify institutions, authority, territory, external relationships, and the meaning of sovereignty relevant to that era.

## Triangulation

When possible, compare different evidence types: inscriptions, archaeological contexts, administrative records, maps, demographic records, oral histories, and modern scholarship. Multiple sources are not independent if they simply repeat the same original claim.

## Legal findings

Quote the court or institution accurately, include the date and paragraph number, distinguish a binding judgment from an advisory opinion or political resolution, and do not extend a finding beyond its stated scope.
